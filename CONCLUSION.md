## 1. Which layer changed your results the most?

Looking at the raw verdict changes, Layer 1 had the biggest impact on this batch, shifting the outcome for 4 out of 24 questions (moving the overall split from 16/4/4 to 18/6/0). However, it is important to look at all three metrics together rather than just celebrating the drop in stopped runs—turning a halted run into a wrong answer does not actually make the system more reliable. Error recovery gives the model another shot at using its tools, but it cannot change the underlying model weights or guarantee that it interprets the data right. Grounding plays a very different role by blocking unsupported claims and occasionally nudging the model into a valid repair. Ultimately, these layers control how the model interacts with tools and what gets accepted as a final output. Because the Wilson confidence intervals overlap in this notebook, I would avoid claiming these minor shifts represent a statistically proven accuracy gain.

## 2. One failure the full harness did not fix

Even with Layer 3 active, question `q08` still failed, returning `'Jambyl'` as the final answer. Although `compare_regions` returned the properly ordered rows, the model simply picked the wrong region for the ranking requested. Because `'Jambyl'` actually appeared in the tool output, the grounding check saw valid evidence for the name and let it pass, completely missing the fact that it grabbed the wrong end of the list. To fix this, I would build a deterministic handler specifically for standard ranking queries: validate the indicator and year first, then programmatically pull the first or last row based on the requested sort order. While this means writing and maintaining standard code and unit tests, it is much safer than relying on another LLM judgment call. Strict parameter validation remains critical here, since pulling the right row for the wrong year or metric would just generate a very confident wrong answer.

## 3. Workflow or agent for the monthly ranking?

For a recurring monthly highest-and-lowest brief, I would go with a fixed workflow since the structure of the task is completely predictable. Across the 15 fixed-shape test questions, the workflow scored 13/15 compared to the bare agent's 9/15. The efficiency gap is even clearer: the workflow needed just 15 calls, 3,852 tokens, and 16.6 seconds, whereas the agent burned through 26 calls, 19,835 tokens, and 75.3 seconds. Even though overlapping confidence intervals keep us from declaring a definitive statistical winner on accuracy, the speed and token savings make the workflow the obvious choice. For production, I would expand that deterministic template to extract both ends of the ranking at once and flag any missing data or invalid inputs for manual review. Keep in mind these latency numbers are just benchmark snapshots rather than strict SLAs. I would save the agentic approach for open-ended queries that genuinely need flexible tool selection or multi-step reasoning that a rigid pipeline cannot handle.
